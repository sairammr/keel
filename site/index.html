The number that leaks

Your liquidation bot has a number — the health factor at which it acts. The official starter puts that number inside a trusted enclave and calls it private. It is not. Every action the bot takes, and every action it declines to take, answers a yes-or-no question about the threshold: it acted at 1.09, so the trigger lies below; it held at 1.06, so the trigger lies above. Each round of the scenario is one probe of a binary search that somebody else is running against you.

Three actions locate a fixed threshold to within ±0.005 HF. Combine that with the wallet reserve — balanceOf, public all along — and the “private” strategy becomes a stop-hunt target: push the price to just above the located trigger, drain the defence, then take the position.

With the challenge’s real parameters the entire game is played inside a $205 window. The on-chain test is an integer (hf ≤ 100 liquidates), so a true health factor of 1.009 already falls. Keel treats 1.01 as the line and keeps an emergency override above it.

A trigger that refuses to be a number

So Keel’s trigger is not a number. It is a distribution:

trigger = clamp(base + k·σ + jitter(HMAC(salt, round)))

One fresh draw per price level, keyed by a sealed salt and the round index. The draw is upward-only: it can make Keel act earlier, never later, so the cleverness never costs survival. A hard floor and an emergency override sit beneath all of it.

The adversary is welcome to know the algorithm — Kerckhoffs’ principle, and our own hunter model knows it exactly. It still cannot aim: no sequence of observed actions narrows base below the jitter width, because every observation is explained equally well by a lower base with a high draw or a higher base with a low one. The secrecy rests on the salt, not on the code being unknown.

Upward only

A jitter draw moves the trigger toward safety, never past the floor. Acting early costs basis points; acting late costs the position.

Proof without disclosure

Secrecy is cheap to claim. Keel’s difference is that the secrecy is verifiable: the policy provably existed before the storm and provably never changed, without being shown to anyone.

Before the scenario starts, the enclave commits keccak256(policyHash ‖ salt) to Sepolia. Every defend then carries an EIP-712 receipt signed by a key derived inside the enclave from the same salt: the action lands at nonce n, its receipt at n+1, from the same account. The pairing is visible on Etherscan, and the receipt schema carries no trigger fields, so the receipts themselves leak nothing.

After the scenario ends, Keel reveals (policyBytes, salt). From there, anyone can recompute the commitment, re-derive every round’s trigger from public PriceUpdate logs, and check each action, and each deliberate inaction, against the sealed policy. The independent verifier goes further: it refuses to trust the receipts’ self-reported health factors and reconstructs each round’s pre-action HF from chain logs on its own. A mismatch would print RECEIPT HF MISMATCH.

The storm it survived

This is not a simulation of a crash. On Sepolia, against a byte-for-byte faithful copy of the official challenge contract, the same price crash hit two positions. The undefended one fell through the liquidation line at round 3 and was liquidated. Keel deposited at rounds 1 and 3, never dropped below 1.10, and finished the storm with its debt intact.

Sealed in the enclave

The controller runs under Chainlink CRE as a confidential handlerInTee workflow. Four secrets (the 13-parameter policy, the salt, an RPC credential, the wallet key) are fetched inside an attested AWS Nitro enclave and exist nowhere else: not in a config file, not in a log, not in this repository. Each tick is one batched chain read, a decision, and one batched send — two HTTP requests of a five-request quota. The handler returns a single word.
